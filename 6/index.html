Part A: Playing with Diffusion Models

In this part, I experimented with pretrained diffusion models (DeepFloyd IF) and implemented sampling, denoising, inpainting, and visual illusion generation. The goal was to understand the mechanics of diffusion, iterative denoising, and classifier-free guidance (CFG). Below are my results and analyses for each section.

1.1 Forward Process

I implemented the forward diffusion process, which progressively adds Gaussian noise to the image at specified timesteps. Below are the noisy versions of the Berkeley Campanile image.

1.2 Classical Denoising

I applied Gaussian blur to attempt to denoise the noisy Campanile images. As expected, the results were poor — classical filters cannot recover fine details lost in heavy Gaussian noise.

1.3 One-Step Denoising

Using the pretrained UNet denoiser, I performed one-step denoising for each noise level. This produced noticeably better reconstructions compared to Gaussian blur, though still imperfect at higher noise levels.


1.4 Iterative Denoising

I implemented iterative denoising using a strided timestep schedule. The image gradually becomes clearer as the diffusion process progresses. This approach outperformed one-step denoising and classical filtering.

1.5 Diffusion Model Sampling

I generated random samples from pure noise using my iterative denoising loop. The prompt used was "a high quality photo". These early results show recognizable but imperfect natural images.

1.6 Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG)

Implemented CFG to enhance image fidelity by combining conditional and unconditional denoising predictions. This significantly improved realism and prompt adherence at the cost of diversity.

1.7 Image-to-Image Translation (SDEdit)

I added small amounts of noise to the Campanile image and iteratively denoised with CFG. Lower noise levels preserved structure, while higher noise levels created more dramatic “edits.”

1.7.1 Editing Hand-Drawn and Web Images

I tested SDEdit on both web images and two hand-drawn sketches. As noise increased, the model “realized” my drawings into photorealistic interpretations.

Part B: Flow Matching from Scratch!

In this part of the project, I trained several UNet architectures on the MNIST dataset to perform flow matching. I first built a simple one-step denoiser, then extended it with time conditioning and finally class conditioning. The goal was to understand the flow matching framework and how conditioning improves generative quality.

1.1 Implementing the UNet

I implemented a UNet with downsampling and upsampling blocks, skip connections, and convolutional layers following the architecture diagram provided. The network was designed to take a noisy MNIST image and output its denoised version. Below is the schematic architecture I used.

1.2 Visualizing the Noising Process

Before training, I visualized the noising process for several values of the noise parameter σ. As expected, higher σ values produced progressively noisier digits.

1.2.1 Training the Single-Step Denoiser

I trained the UNet on the MNIST training set for 5 epochs with Adam optimizer (lr=1e-4). The training objective minimized the L2 reconstruction loss between clean and denoised images. The model gradually learned to recover the underlying digit structure from noisy inputs.

1.2.2 Out-of-Distribution Testing

1.2.3 Denoising Pure Noise

After several epochs, outputs resemble blurry averages of MNIST digits. Because the model minimizes MSE over all training digits, it learns the pixel-wise mean of the dataset — roughly the centroid of all digits — rather than distinct numbers. This is expected behavior for MSE loss.

Part 2: Training a Flow Matching Model

After exploring simple denoising, I trained a time-conditioned UNet to predict the flow between noisy and clean samples. Flow matching allows iterative denoising and true generative sampling.

2.1 Adding Time Conditioning to UNet

I modified the UNet to accept a scalar timestep t, normalized to [0,1]. This value was embedded via fully-connected layers and modulated intermediate UNet activations as described in the spec. Below is a schematic illustration of the conditioning mechanism.

2.2 Training the Time-Conditioned UNet

I trained the time-conditioned model with batch size 64 for several epochs. The exponential learning rate scheduler (γ=0.95) improved stability. The model learned to predict the flow between clean and noisy distributions effectively.

2.3 Sampling from the Time-Conditioned UNet

I used the trained UNet to iteratively denoise pure noise and generate MNIST-like samples. The quality improved steadily over epochs, producing recognizable digits after 10 epochs.

2.4 Adding Class Conditioning to UNet

To improve sample quality and control, I added class conditioning via one-hot vectors. Each digit class was embedded through two additional fully-connected blocks and combined with the time conditioning signal. Dropout (10%) was used for classifier-free guidance.

2.5 Training the Class-Conditioned UNet

Training followed the same setup as the time-conditioned model but now included class-conditioning vectors. The model converged faster and achieved better perceptual quality.

2.6 Sampling from the Class-Conditioned UNet

The class-conditioned model produced the best results. After 10 epochs, generated digits were clean and diverse. I generated 4 samples per class (digits 0–9) for comparison.

Removing the Learning Rate Scheduler

To simplify training, I removed the exponential scheduler and compensated by slightly reducing the base learning rate and using gradient clipping to prevent instability. The final model maintained similar performance while eliminating unnecessary complexity.

Reflection

This part of the project solidified my understanding of diffusion-like training through flow matching. Implementing UNets with time and class conditioning demonstrated how conditioning guides generative behavior and stabilizes learning. I also observed firsthand how architectural and training design choices, such as skip connections, conditioning, and scheduler tuning, influence convergence and quality.
